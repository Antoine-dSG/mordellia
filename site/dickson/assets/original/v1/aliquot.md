<!-- passage:catalan -->
E. Catalan<sup>374</sup> stated empirically that if $n_1$ is the sum of the divisors $<n$ of $n$, and $n_2$ is the sum of the divisors $<n_1$ of $n_1$, etc., then $n,n_1,n_2,\ldots$ have a limit $\lambda$, where $\lambda$ is unity or a perfect number.

J. Perrott<sup>375</sup> [Perott] noted that there is no limit for $n=220$, since

$$n_1=n_3=\cdots=284,\qquad n_2=n_4=\cdots=220.$$

<div class="historical-note"><sup>374</sup> Bull. Soc. Math. France, 16, 1887–8, 129. Mathesis, 8, 1888, 130.<br><sup>375</sup> Ibid., 17, 1888–9, 155–6.</div>

<!-- passage:poulet -->
P. Poulet<sup>387</sup> discovered the chain of period five,

$$n=12496=2^4\cdot11\cdot71,\quad s(n)=2^4\cdot19\cdot47,\quad s^2(n)=2^4\cdot967,\quad s^3(n)=2^3\cdot23\cdot79,\quad s^4(n)=2^3\cdot1783,$$

with $s^5(n)=n$; and noted that 14316 leads a chain of 28 terms.

<div class="historical-note"><sup>387</sup> L'intermédiaire des math., 25, 1918, 100–1.</div>

<!-- passage:equal-images -->
Daniel Schwenter<sup>52</sup> noted in 1636 that 27 and 35 have the same sum of aliquot parts. Kraft<sup>363</sup> noted in 1749 that this is true of the pairs $45,3\cdot29$; $39,55$; $93,145$; and $45,13\cdot19$. In 1823, Thomas Taylor<sup>102</sup> called two such numbers imperfectly amicable, citing the pairs 27, 35; 39, 55; 65, 77; 51, 91; 95, 119; 69, 133; 115, 187; 87, 247. George Peacock<sup>400</sup> used the same term.

<div class="historical-note"><sup>400</sup> Encyclopaedia Metropolitana, London, I, 1845, 422.</div>

<!-- passage:triples -->
L. E. Dickson<sup>403</sup> defined an amicable triple to be three numbers such that the sum of the aliquot divisors of each equals the sum of the remaining two numbers. After developing a theory analogous to that by Euler<sup>364</sup> for amicable numbers, Dickson obtained eight sets of amicable triples in which two of the numbers are equal, and two triples of distinct numbers:

$$\begin{array}{lll}293\cdot337a,&5\cdot16561a,&99371a\qquad(a=2^5\cdot3\cdot13),\\3\cdot89b,&11\cdot29b,&359b\qquad(b=2^{14}\cdot5\cdot19\cdot31\cdot151).\end{array}$$

<div class="historical-note"><sup>403</sup> Amer. Math. Monthly, 20, 1913, 84–92.</div>
