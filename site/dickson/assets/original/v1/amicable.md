<!-- passage:definition -->
Two numbers are called amicable* if each equals the sum of the aliquot divisors of the other.

<div class="historical-note">* Amiable, agreeable, befreundete, verwandte.</div>

<!-- passage:thabit -->
In the ninth century the Arab Thâbit ben Korrah<sup>10</sup> (prop. 10) noted that $2^nht$ and $2^ns$ are amicable numbers if

$$\tag{1}h=3\cdot2^n-1,\qquad t=3\cdot2^{n-1}-1,\qquad s=9\cdot2^{2n-1}-1$$

are primes $>2$, literally, if

$$h=z+2^n,\quad t=z-2^{n-1},\quad z=1+2+\cdots+2^n,\quad s=(2^{n+1}+2^{n-2})2^{n+1}-1.$$

The term used for amicable numbers was *se invicem amantes*. In the article in which F. Woepcke<sup>10</sup> translated this Arabic manuscript into French, he noted that a definition of these numbers, called *congeneres*, occurs in the 51st treatise (on arithmetic) of Ikhovân Alçafâ, manuscript 1105, anciens fonds arabes, p. 15, of the National Library of Paris.

<!-- passage:descartes -->
Descartes<sup>355</sup> gave the rule: Take (2 or) any power of 2 such that its triple less 1, its sextuple less 1, and the 18-fold of its square less 1 are all primes;* the product of the last prime by the double of the assumed power of 2 is one of a pair of amicable numbers. Starting with the powers 2, 8, 64, we get 284, 18416, 9437056, whose aliquot parts make 220, etc. Thus the third pair is

$$9363584=2^7\cdot191\cdot383,\qquad9437056=2^7\cdot73727.$$

Descartes<sup>356</sup> stated that Fermat's rule agrees exactly with his own.
