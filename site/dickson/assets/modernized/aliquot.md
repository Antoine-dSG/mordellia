## Catalan’s empirical claim and Perrott’s objection

Let $s(n)$ be the sum of the proper positive divisors of $n$. Catalan<sup>374</sup> empirically claims that the sequence $n,s(n),s^2(n),\ldots$ has a limit, either 1 or a perfect number. Superscripts here mean repeated application, not powers.

Perrott<sup>375</sup> [Perott] points out that there is no limit for $n=220$: the odd iterates are 284 and the even iterates are 220.

## Poulet’s longer cycles

Poulet<sup>387</sup> discovers a cycle of length five:

$$n=12496=2^4\cdot11\cdot71,\quad s(n)=2^4\cdot19\cdot47,\quad s^2(n)=2^4\cdot967,$$
$$s^3(n)=2^3\cdot23\cdot79,\quad s^4(n)=2^3\cdot1783,\quad s^5(n)=n.$$

He also notes that 14316 begins a cycle of 28 terms.

<div class="historical-note"><sup>387</sup> L'intermédiaire des math., 25, 1918, 100–1.</div>

## Equal proper-divisor sums

Schwenter<sup>52</sup> notes in 1636 that $s(27)=s(35)$. Kraft<sup>363</sup> gives the pairs $(45,87)$, $(39,55)$, $(93,145)$ and $(45,247)$ in 1749. In 1823, Taylor<sup>102</sup> calls pairs with the same proper-divisor sum **imperfectly amicable**, listing $(27,35)$, $(39,55)$, $(65,77)$, $(51,91)$, $(95,119)$, $(69,133)$, $(115,187)$ and $(87,247)$. Peacock<sup>400</sup> uses the same term.

<div class="historical-note"><sup>400</sup> Encyclopaedia Metropolitana, London, I, 1845, 422.</div>

## Amicable triples

Dickson<sup>403</sup> defines an amicable triple $(A,B,C)$ by

$$s(A)=B+C,\qquad s(B)=A+C,\qquad s(C)=A+B.$$

He develops a theory analogous to Euler's<sup>364</sup> for pairs, and obtains eight triples with two equal entries and two with distinct entries:

$$a(293\cdot337,\ 5\cdot16561,\ 99371),\qquad a=2^5\cdot3\cdot13,$$
$$b(3\cdot89,\ 11\cdot29,\ 359),\qquad b=2^{14}\cdot5\cdot19\cdot31\cdot151.$$

<div class="historical-note"><sup>403</sup> Amer. Math. Monthly, 20, 1913, 84–92.</div>
