    Bounds on prime factors · Selected source excerpt · p. 30

    T. M. Putnam182 proved that not all of the $r$ distinct prime factors of a perfect number exceed $1+r/\log_e2$ and hence do not all equal or exceed $1+3r/2$.

    182 Amer. Math. Monthly, 17, 1910, 167.

    Dickson’s converse · Selected source excerpt · p. 30

    L. E. Dickson183 gave an immediate proof that every even perfect number is of Euclid's type. Let $2^nq$ be perfect, where $q$ is odd and $n>0$. Then $(2^{n+1}-1)s=2^{n+1}q$, where $s$ is the sum of all the divisors of $q$. Thus $s=q+d$, where $d=q/(2^{n+1}-1)$. Hence $d$ is an integral divisor of $q$, so that $q$ and $d$ are the only divisors of $q$. Hence $d=1$ and $q$ is a prime.

    183 Ibid., 18, 1911, 109.

    Primitive non-deficient numbers · Selected source excerpt · p. 31

    L. E. Dickson193 called a non-deficient number primitive if it is not a multiple of a smaller non-deficient number, and proved that there is only a finite number of primitive non-deficient numbers having a given number of distinct odd prime factors and a given number of factors 2. As a corollary, there is not an infinitude of odd perfect numbers with any given number of distinct prime factors. There is no odd abundant number with fewer than three distinct prime factors; the primitive ones with three are

    $$3^3 5\cdot7,\quad 3^2 5^2 7,\quad 3^2 5\cdot7^2,\quad 3^3 5^2 11,\quad 3^5 5^2 13,\quad 3^4 5^3 13,\quad 3^4 5^2 13^2,\quad 3^3 5^3 13^2.$$

    There is given a list of the numerous primitive odd abundant numbers with four distinct prime factors and lists of even non-deficient numbers of certain types. In particular, all primitive non-deficient numbers $<15000$ are determined (23 odd and 78 even). In view of these lists, there is no odd perfect number with four or fewer distinct prime factors (cf. Sylvester148–153).

    193 Amer. Jour. Math., 35, 1913, 413–26.

    Bounds on prime factors · Selected excerpt, modernized · p. 30

    A bound on the least prime factor

    Putnam182 proves that if a perfect number has $r$ distinct prime factors, at least one is at most $1+r/\log 2$. Consequently they cannot all be at least $1+3r/2$.

    182 Amer. Math. Monthly, 17, 1910, 167.

    Dickson’s converse · Selected excerpt, modernized · p. 30

    Dickson’s converse

    Dickson183 gives a short proof of the classification of even perfect numbers. Suppose $2^a m$ is perfect, where $m$ is odd and $a>0$. Let $\sigma(m)$ be the sum of all positive divisors of $m$. Then

    $$(2^{a+1}-1)\sigma(m)=2^{a+1}m.$$

    Thus $\sigma(m)=m+d$, where $d=m/(2^{a+1}-1)$. The integer $d$ divides $m$. Since the sum of all divisors is already $m+d$, these are its only divisors. Therefore $d=1$ and $m$ is prime.

    183 Ibid., 18, 1911, 109.

    Primitive non-deficient numbers · Selected excerpt, modernized · p. 31

    Primitive non-deficient numbers

    Dickson193 calls a number non-deficient when $\sigma(n)\ge2n$, and primitive when none of its proper divisors is non-deficient. He proves that fixing the number of distinct odd prime factors and the exponent of 2 leaves only finitely many primitive non-deficient numbers. The corollary is that there are only finitely many odd perfect numbers with a fixed number of distinct prime factors.

    He reports that an odd abundant number has at least three distinct prime factors, and gives the following primitive ones with exactly three:

    $$3^3\cdot5\cdot7,\quad3^2\cdot5^2\cdot7,\quad3^2\cdot5\cdot7^2,\quad3^3\cdot5^2\cdot11,$$ $$3^5\cdot5^2\cdot13,\quad3^4\cdot5^3\cdot13,\quad3^4\cdot5^2\cdot13^2,\quad3^3\cdot5^3\cdot13^2.$$

    His paper lists many primitive odd abundant numbers with four distinct prime factors, and even non-deficient numbers of specified types. It determines all primitive non-deficient numbers below 15000: 23 odd and 78 even. Dickson concludes from these lists that no odd perfect number has four or fewer distinct prime factors, and compares Sylvester.148–153

    193 Amer. Jour. Math., 35, 1913, 413–26.

    Reinterpreted

    Divisor sums relative to size

    For $n\ge1$, define the abundancy ratio

    $$A(n)=\frac{\sigma(n)}n=\sum_{d\mid n}\frac1d.$$

    The second equality follows because $d\mapsto n/d$ permutes the divisors. From the divisor product,

    $$A(n)=\prod_{p^a\parallel n} L_p(a),\qquad L_p(a)=1+\frac1p+\cdots+\frac1{p^a}.$$

    The notation $p^a\parallel n$ means that $a$ is the exact exponent of $p$. For every finite $a$,

    $$L_p(a)<\frac p{p-1}.$$

    Proposition. An odd integer with at most two distinct prime factors is deficient.

    Proof. For one prime factor the bound is at most $3/2<2$. For two, the largest possible upper bound uses the smallest odd primes, 3 and 5:

    $$A(n)<\frac32\frac54=\frac{15}{8}<2.$$

    Thus arbitrary large exponents cannot overcome a shortage of prime factors. The argument explains the kind of restriction in Dickson's p. 31 report; it does not prove that report's stronger four-prime exclusion.

    Putnam's bound on p. 30 follows from the same product with logarithms. If $n$ is perfect with distinct primes $p_1,\ldots,p_r$, then

    $$\log2=\log A(n)<\sum_{i=1}^r\log\left(1+\frac1{p_i-1}\right)<\sum_{i=1}^r\frac1{p_i-1}.$$

    Here $\log(1+x)<x$ for $x>0$ follows by integrating $1/t<1$ from 1 to $1+x$. If every $p_i>1+r/\log2$, the right side would be less than $\log2$, a contradiction. Thus some $p_i\le1+r/\log2$. Also $\log2>2/3$: the function $\log x-2(x-1)/(x+1)$ is zero at 1 and has derivative $(x-1)^2/[x(x+1)^2]>0$ for $x>1$. This gives Putnam's weaker bound $p_i<1+3r/2$. The logarithmic proof is optional for subsequent units; the finite-product bound is all they need.

    Positivity and divisor containment

    Lemma. If $m\mid n$ and $m<n$, then $A(m)<A(n)$.

    Proof. Every divisor of $m$ is a divisor of $n$, and $n$ itself is an additional divisor. In the reciprocal sums defining $A$, all summands are positive.

    Consequently every proper multiple of a perfect number is abundant and every proper divisor of a perfect number is deficient. The qualifier proper matters: the number itself is neither a deficient divisor nor an abundant multiple of itself.

    Dickson's short converse uses positivity differently. If the sum of all positive divisors is already the sum of two distinct known divisors, there is no room for a third. Divisor containment proves monotonicity; exhaustion of a divisor sum proves the converse. Neither needs estimates about large primes.

    Inspect the local factors

    Primitive non-deficient numbers

    A number is non-deficient when $A(n)\ge2$. It is primitive non-deficient if all of its proper divisors are deficient. Every non-deficient number has a primitive non-deficient divisor: choose the smallest non-deficient divisor from its finite divisor set. Thus these primitive numbers generate the non-deficient numbers by divisibility.

    One can check primitiveness efficiently once the factorization is known. It suffices to check $A(n/p)<2$ for every prime $p\mid n$. Every proper divisor divides at least one such $n/p$, and monotonicity handles all smaller divisors.

    For example, $945=3^3\cdot5\cdot7$ has $\sigma(945)=40\cdot6\cdot8=1920>1890$. Removing one factor of 3, 5 or 7 gives $315,189,135$, each deficient. So 945 is primitive abundant.

    Dickson reports a further theorem: with the number of distinct odd prime factors and the exponent of 2 fixed, there are only finitely many primitive non-deficient numbers. This theorem is an external input here; the monotonicity argument is not its proof. Every perfect number is primitive, so his stated corollary for odd perfect numbers follows from it. The eight three-prime examples and historical bounds remain in Modernized. Their exact arithmetic can be checked without turning those checks into an exhaustive classification proof.

    Relation to the source

    The normalized divisor sum unifies abundance, proper-divisor restrictions and prime-support estimates. It makes the primitive condition operational without replacing Dickson's finiteness theorem by an unjustified finite search. The essential prerequisites are the divisor product and positive finite sums; the optional Putnam derivation additionally uses logarithms and elementary calculus. No new invariant is claimed: $\sigma(n)/n$ and the reciprocal-divisor identity are classical, and occur in the chapter's calculations.
