    Two numbers are called amicable* if each equals the sum of the aliquot divisors of the other.

    * Amiable, agreeable, befreundete, verwandte.

    Thabit’s construction · Selected source excerpt · p. 39 (printed 36)

    In the ninth century the Arab Thâbit ben Korrah10 (prop. 10) noted that $2^nht$ and $2^ns$ are amicable numbers if

    $$\tag{1}h=3\cdot2^n-1,\qquad t=3\cdot2^{n-1}-1,\qquad s=9\cdot2^{2n-1}-1$$

    are primes $>2$, literally, if

    $$h=z+2^n,\quad t=z-2^{n-1},\quad z=1+2+\cdots+2^n,\quad s=(2^{n+1}+2^{n-2})2^{n+1}-1.$$

    The term used for amicable numbers was se invicem amantes. In the article in which F. Woepcke10 translated this Arabic manuscript into French, he noted that a definition of these numbers, called congeneres, occurs in the 51st treatise (on arithmetic) of Ikhovân Alçafâ, manuscript 1105, anciens fonds arabes, p. 15, of the National Library of Paris.

    The three early pairs · Selected source excerpt · p. 40

    Descartes355 gave the rule: Take (2 or) any power of 2 such that its triple less 1, its sextuple less 1, and the 18-fold of its square less 1 are all primes;* the product of the last prime by the double of the assumed power of 2 is one of a pair of amicable numbers. Starting with the powers 2, 8, 64, we get 284, 18416, 9437056, whose aliquot parts make 220, etc. Thus the third pair is

    $$9363584=2^7\cdot191\cdot383,\qquad9437056=2^7\cdot73727.$$

    Descartes356 stated that Fermat's rule agrees exactly with his own.

    355 Oeuvres de Descartes, 2, 1898, 93–94, letter to Mersenne, Mar. 31, 1638.
    * Evidently the numbers (1) if the initial power of 2 be $2^{n-1}$.
    356 Oeuvres de Descartes, 2, 1898, 148, letter to Mersenne, May 27, 1638.

    Amicable numbers

    Two positive integers $M,N$ are amicable if $s(M)=N$ and $s(N)=M$, where $s$ is the sum of proper positive divisors. Dickson also records amiable, agreeable, befreundete, and verwandte.

    * Amiable, agreeable, befreundete, verwandte.

    Thabit’s construction · Selected excerpt, modernized · p. 39 (printed 36)

    Thabit’s construction

    Dickson reports that Thabit ben Korrah10 gives the amicable pair $2^nht,2^nr$ when

    $$h=3\cdot2^n-1,\qquad t=3\cdot2^{n-1}-1,\qquad r=9\cdot2^{2n-1}-1$$

    are all primes greater than 2. In the literal formulation, put

    $$z=1+2+\cdots+2^n,\quad h=z+2^n,\quad t=z-2^{n-1},\quad r=(2^{n+1}+2^{n-2})2^{n+1}-1.$$

    The Latin term is se invicem amantes. In his French translation of this Arabic manuscript, Woepcke10 notes a definition under the name congeneres in the arithmetic treatise (number 51) of Ikhovân Alçafâ, manuscript 1105, anciens fonds arabes, p. 15, National Library of Paris.

    The three early pairs · Selected excerpt, modernized · p. 40

    Descartes’ formulation

    Descartes355 takes a power of 2, $t\ge2$, such that $3t-1$, $6t-1$ and $18t^2-1$ are prime. Then $2t(18t^2-1)$ is one member of an amicable pair. The initial powers $2,8,64$ give $284,18416,9437056$; their proper-divisor sums give the other members, starting with 220. The third pair is

    $$9363584=2^7\cdot191\cdot383,\qquad9437056=2^7\cdot73727.$$

    Dickson identifies this with formula (1) on taking $t=2^{n-1}$. Descartes356 says that Fermat's rule agrees exactly with his own.

    Reinterpreted

    A common total divisor sum

    Two distinct positive integers $M,N$ form an amicable pair if $s(M)=N$ and $s(N)=M$. Equivalently,

    $$\sigma(M)=\sigma(N)=M+N.$$

    Indeed, subtracting $M$ or $N$ from this equality gives the two defining equations. If equal entries are allowed, the condition reduces to a repeated perfect number; we separate that case from pairs of distinct numbers.

    For $220=2^2\cdot5\cdot11$ and $284=2^2\cdot71$,

    $$\sigma(220)=7\cdot6\cdot12=504=7\cdot72=\sigma(284),$$

    and $220+284=504$. Equality of the two divisor sums alone is insufficient: their common value must also equal the sum of the two numbers.

    The normalized sums satisfy a useful identity, reported by Haas on p. 48:

    $$\frac1{A(M)}+\frac1{A(N)}=\frac M{M+N}+\frac N{M+N}=1.$$

    If $M<N$, then $A(M)>2$ and $A(N)<2$: the smaller member is abundant and the larger is deficient. Thus equal total divisor sums coexist with different abundancy ratios.

    Thabit’s conditional construction

    Proposition. Let $n\ge2$. If

    $$p=3\cdot2^{n-1}-1,\qquad q=3\cdot2^n-1,\qquad r=9\cdot2^{2n-1}-1$$

    are all prime, then $M=2^npq$ and $N=2^nr$ are amicable.

    Proof. Put $t=2^{n-1}$. Then $p=3t-1$, $q=6t-1$, $r=18t^2-1$, and $2^n=2t$. The primes are distinct and odd, so

    $$\sigma(M)=(4t-1)(p+1)(q+1)=(4t-1)18t^2=\sigma(N).$$

    Meanwhile

    $$M+N=2t\big[(3t-1)(6t-1)+(18t^2-1)\big]=18t^2(4t-1).$$

    The common-sum criterion proves the claim.

    The condition $n\ge2$ matters. For $n=1$, $p=2$ would overlap the power-of-two factor, so the divisor-product calculation used in the proof would be wrong. Nor is primality automatic: for $n=3$, $r=287=7\cdot41$. The construction is not a proof of infinitely many pairs.

    The same construction in different parameters

    Descartes starts from the power $t$; Thabit's formula uses $n$ with $t=2^{n-1}$. This change of parameter produces exactly the same three prime tests and pair:

    $n$ $t$ $p,q,r$ Pair
    2 2 $5,11,71$ $220,284$
    4 8 $23,47,1151$ $17296,18416$
    7 64 $191,383,73727$ $9363584,9437056$

    This is an explicit equivalence of formulas, not evidence about which historical author first knew every part of the method. Dickson's attribution and correspondence notices supply the historical account.

    A broader construction needs to vary the common factor as well as the remaining primes. Euler's five problems do this. They also expose why a successful prime formula need not exhaust amicable pairs.

    Relation to the source

    The common-total criterion explains Thabit's formula in two calculations. Reinterpreted adds the proof absent from the selected statement and distinguishes equal-entry perfect numbers from distinct amicable pairs. It preserves all prime and coprimality conditions, and makes the parameter change to Descartes explicit. The only background is the divisor product developed earlier; no claims of infinitude or complete classification follow.
