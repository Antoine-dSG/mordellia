Original

9. Applications to billiard ball problem.

As has been seen, there are no points of the ring which are invariant under $T$. On the other hand consider $T^2$ followed by a rotation of the $\theta,\varphi$ plane through an angle $-2\pi$, which we designate by $R_{-1}$. The resultant transformation of the ring admits the same area integral as $T$ of course, but advances the points of the outer circle by an angle $2\pi$, and those of the inner circle by an angle $-2\pi$ of opposite sign. These are the two conditions essential for the application of Poincaré's geometric theorem. Hence $T^2R_{-1}$ (the compound transformation) possesses two invariant points. This means that $T^2$ has two geometrically distinct invariant points of oppositely signed indices,* although these correspond to an increase of $2\pi$ for $\varphi$.

* See my paper An Extension of Poincaré's Last Geometric Theorem, Acta Mathematica, vol. 47 (1926). By the index of an invariant point is meant the number of positive rotations of a line joining a point $P$ to its image $P_1$, when $P$ makes a small positive circuit of the invariant point.

If $P$ is such an invariant point, so is $T(P)$ of course but with the same index. Thus we get two pairs of points, say

$$P,\ T(P),\ Q,\ T(Q),$$

all four distinct. These evidently correspond to the two fundamental periodic motions.

For the application of the theorem of Poincaré to the periodic motions of more complicated type, it is necessary to take account of the fact that every such motion is associated with a distinct second such motion, obtained by reversing the direction of description, although these motions have the same index. However one of these motions increases $\varphi$ by $2k\pi$, the other increases it by $2(n-k)\pi$. By only considering invariant points of $T^n$ for which $\varphi$ increases by $2k\pi$, $(k\le n/2)$, we clearly obtain each harmonic $n$-sided polygon only once. It may be noted in passing that this pairing of motions in the billiard ball problem is fully reflected in the fact that $T$ is a product of two involutory transformations; it was the same special property of the ring transformation in the restricted problem of three bodies which enabled me to prove the existence of infinitely many symmetric periodic orbits.*

* See my paper The Restricted Problem of Three Bodies, Rendiconti di Palermo, vol. 39 (1915), in particular, section 14.

Now turn to the invariant points of the compound transformation $T^nR_{-k}$ where $R_{-k}$ denotes $k$-fold rotation through the angle $-2\pi$. The rotations on the outer and inner circles are clearly

$$2(n-k)\pi\quad\text{and}\quad-2k\pi,$$

which will be of opposite sign. Thus we can infer the existence of at least two geometrically distinct series of points

$$P,\ T(P),\ldots,\ T^{n-1}(P),\qquad Q,\ T(Q),\ldots,\ T^{n-1}(Q)$$

such that $T^n(P)=P$, $T^n(Q)=Q$, while $\varphi$ has been increased by $2k\pi$; it is assumed that $k$ and $n$ are relatively prime.

To prove this assertion in detail, we may let $P$ be one such invariant point, such that $T^n$ increases $\varphi$ by $2k\pi$. If the $n$ points

$$P,\ T(P),\ldots,\ T^{n-1}(P)$$

are not distinct, let $T^m(P)=P$, $(m\le n-1)$, and suppose that $\varphi$ is increased by $2j\pi$. By combination of the two symbolic equations $T^m(P)=P$, $T^n(P)=P$, we obtain $T^d(P)=P$ where $d$ ($\ne1$ of course) is the greatest common divisor of $m$ and $n$. Thus $P$ is invariant under $T^d$. Suppose that under $T^d$, the $\varphi$ of $P$ increases by $2f\pi$. From the equation $T^n=T^{qd}$, we see that $T^n$ will then increase the $\varphi$ of $P$ by $2qf\pi$, so that $k=qf$. Thus $k$ and $n$ would possess a common factor $q$, contrary to hypothesis.

Not only are the $n$ points distinct but they have the same index. Hence there will be a point $q$ invariant under $T^n$ and with oppositely signed index. This, with its images under successive powers of $T$, will necessarily be distinct from the points of the set generated by $P$, and leads to a second distinct series of $n$ points.

Hence we obtain for every $n>2$ and every relatively prime $k\le n/2$, two geometrically distinct, harmonic polygons with $n$ sides and making $k$ circuits of the curve $C$. Corresponding to these there will be of course four periodic motions. We shall not attempt to develop here the characteristics as to type of stability and instability, dependent upon the sign of the index.

It is worthy of observation that the method of sections 2, 3 is evidently applicable here to show that there exist infinitely many periodic motions lying in the vicinity of any periodic motion of general stable type if the constant $l$ is not $0$.

We shall indicate in particular how this same method seems to apply to the limiting type of periodic motion in which the billiard ball is rolled around the table.

For this purpose it is essential to examine the explicit formulas given for $T$ in the case when $\theta$ is small. A direct computation leads to the result

$$\theta_1-\theta=\frac{2}{3}\frac{k'}{k^2}\theta^2+l\theta^3+\cdots,$$

$$\varphi_1-\varphi=\frac{2}{k}\theta-\frac{4k'}{3k^3}\theta^2+m\theta^3+\cdots,$$

where the function $k(\varphi)$ denotes the curvature of $C$ at the point with given $\varphi$, and where the functions $l,m,\ldots$ depend on $\varphi$ only. Proceeding entirely formally and replacing $\theta_1-\theta$ and $\varphi_1-\varphi$ by $\Delta\theta$ and $\Delta\varphi$ respectively, we obtain the approximate differential equation

$$\frac{d\theta}{d\varphi}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{k'}{k}\theta,$$

which gives by integration

$$\theta=\theta_0 k^{1/3}(\varphi).$$

Here $\theta_0$ is the value of $\theta$ for a point of curvature unity. This result indicates that, to a first approximation, the curve $\theta=\theta_0 k^{1/3}(\varphi)$ near the inner boundary $\theta=0$ of the ring is nearly invariant under $T$, and can probably be modified by the inclusion of higher order terms so as to be still more nearly invariant. Evidently the limiting periodic motions formed by $C$ are to be regarded as analogous to stable periodic motions on this account.

Also if the variable $n$ represents the number of iterations, we have the approximate differential equation

$$\frac{d\varphi}{dn}=2\theta_0 k^{-2/3},$$

whence by integration

$$n=\int k^{2/3}\,d\varphi/2\theta_0.$$

It follows that $\varphi$ will increase by more than $2\pi$ along the approximately invariant curve if $n\theta_0$ exceeds $\pi K^{2/3}$ where $K$ denotes the maximum curvature of $C$.

It thus appears as highly probable that the lemma of section 2 is applicable and that there exist infinitely many periodic motions uniformly near to $C$.

Modernized

Two-collision trajectories

Use the annulus map of §7 and its real lift $\widetilde T$, whose boundary displacements are $0$ and $2\pi$. Let $D(\theta,s)=(\theta,s+2\pi)$ be the deck translation. The lifted map $D^{-1}\widetilde T^2$ has boundary displacements $-2\pi$ and $2\pi$ and preserves the measure of §8.

Birkhoff applies his extended geometric theorem to obtain fixed points of opposite index. Here the fixed-point index is the winding number of the displacement vector around an isolated fixed point. The cited input is An Extension of Poincaré's Last Geometric Theorem, Acta Mathematica 47 (1926); the opposite-index conclusion is part of that external input, not deduced here from area preservation alone.

If $P$ is such a point, $T(P)$ is also fixed by $T^2$ and has the same index. Since $T$ has no interior fixed points, a pair of opposite-index points $P,Q$ gives four distinct points $P,T(P),Q,T(Q)$, hence two two-collision trajectories.

Reversal and winding

Reversing an $n$-collision trajectory with lifted advance $2k\pi$ gives advance $2(n-k)\pi$ and preserves its index. Restricting to $k\le n/2$ avoids counting the two orientations of the same harmonic polygon twice in the coprime cases considered below. Birkhoff identifies this reversibility with a factorization of $T$ into two involutions, that is, maps whose squares are the identity. He cites the analogous factorization used for symmetric periodic orbits in The Restricted Problem of Three Bodies, Rendiconti di Palermo 39 (1915), §14.

Rational winding and the geometric theorem

For relatively prime integers $k,n$ with $0<k<n$, the map $D^{-k}\widetilde T^n$ has boundary displacements

$$-2k\pi,\qquad 2(n-k)\pi.$$

They have opposite signs. Applying the same extended theorem, Birkhoff obtains fixed points of opposite index. Their projected $T$-orbits have winding $k$ in $n$ collisions. To conclude that these are two distinct $n$-cycles, one must check least period and index along a cycle.

Least period and distinct cycles

Suppose $T^n(P)=P$ with lifted advance $2k\pi$, and also $T^m(P)=P$ for some $m<n$. Set $d=\gcd(m,n)$. Combining the two iterate relations gives $T^d(P)=P$. Since $T$ has no fixed point, $d\ne1$. Write $n=qd$, where $q>1$, and let $2f\pi$ be the advance under $T^d$. Then $k=qf$, so $q$ divides both $k$ and $n$, contradicting coprimality. Thus the $n$ points of the orbit are distinct.

The index of $T^n$ is the same at every point of this cycle. A fixed point of the opposite index therefore belongs to a different cycle. For $n>2$ and $k\le n/2$ coprime to $n$, Birkhoff concludes that there are two distinct harmonic $n$-gons of winding $k$, or four oriented motions after reversal. He does not develop the relation between index sign and stability here.

The stable-orbit cross-reference

Birkhoff invokes §§2–3 for infinitely many periodic motions near a periodic motion of general stable type when the normal-form coefficient denoted there by $l$ is nonzero. He then asks whether the same method applies to the limiting motion along the boundary. This is a cross-reference followed by an exploratory argument, not a new local stability proof in §9.

The formal grazing calculation

Let $\kappa(s)$ be the curvature, with the boundary length still normalized to $2\pi$. For small $\theta$, the source states

$$\theta_1-\theta=\frac{2\kappa'}{3\kappa^2}\theta^2+a(s)\theta^3+\cdots,$$

$$s_1-s=\frac{2}{\kappa}\theta-\frac{4\kappa'}{3\kappa^3}\theta^2+b(s)\theta^3+\cdots.$$

The names $a,b$ replace the source's local coefficients $l,m$; this avoids confusing the cubic coefficient here with the stability coefficient mentioned above. Dividing the leading increments and treating them formally as differentials gives

$$\frac{d\theta}{ds}=\frac{\kappa'}{3\kappa}\theta, \qquad \theta(s)=c\kappa(s)^{1/3}.$$

The constant $c$ is the value of $\theta$ at curvature one, if such a point is used for normalization. Birkhoff interprets these curves as approximately invariant and suggests adding higher-order terms to improve the approximation. He compares the limiting boundary motion to a stable periodic motion. These remain the source's formal inference and proposed improvement.

The formal collision count

Treating the iteration count $N$ as continuous along the approximate curve gives

$$\frac{ds}{dN}=2c\kappa^{-2/3},\qquad N=\frac{1}{2c}\int\kappa^{2/3}\,ds.$$

If $K=\max\kappa$, then $Nc>\pi K^{2/3}$ suffices for an advance greater than $2\pi$ in this model. Birkhoff regards this as making it highly probable that the lemma of §2 applies and that infinitely many periodic motions lie uniformly near the boundary. The calculation retains that provisional conclusion; it does not turn the differential model into a theorem about the discrete map.

Reinterpreted

The closed annulus and its lift

Use the collision map and reflection convention for a bounded strictly convex plane table with regular analytic boundary and strictly positive curvature. Let $L$ be the boundary length, $s\in\mathbb R/L\mathbb Z$ counterclockwise arclength, and $\theta\in(0,\pi)$ the outgoing angle measured from the positive tangent toward the interior. Define normalized coordinates by

$$x=s/L\pmod 1,\qquad y=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}.$$

The physical state space becomes $\mathcal A^\circ=(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)\times(0,1)$. In these coordinates denote the billiard map by $f$. Its continuous extension to $\mathcal A=(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z)\times[0,1]$ fixes both boundary circles pointwise: a chord becomes tangent and its two endpoints coalesce as $\theta\to0$ or $\theta\to\pi$. The extension is a homeomorphism, by the same argument for the inverse map. Boundary points are limiting states, not physical flights.

The invariant measure takes the form

$$d\mu=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,ds=2L\,dx\,dy.$$

Thus $f$ preserves ordinary area on this closed annulus. It preserves orientation in the interior, hence globally, and preserves each boundary component.

Definition (lift). Let $\pi:\widetilde{\mathcal A}=\mathbb R\times[0,1]\to\mathcal A$ send $(x,y)$ to $(x\bmod1,y)$. A lift of $f$ is a continuous map $F:\widetilde{\mathcal A}\to\widetilde{\mathcal A}$ with $\pi F=f\pi$. Choose its first coordinate $F_1$ so that every interior flight satisfies

$$0<F_1(x,y)-x<1.$$

This selects the positive boundary arc from the starting collision to the next collision. It determines a unique lift, with $F(x+1,y)=F(x,y)+(1,0)$. Near $\theta=0$ that arc shrinks; near $\theta=\pi$ its complement shrinks. Consequently,

$$F(x,0)=(x,0),\qquad F(x,1)=(x+1,1).$$

Both circles are fixed on the annulus, but their lifts differ by one full turn. The distinction is essential to counting winding.

Winding, periodic states, and periodic orbits

A state $z\in\mathcal A^\circ$ is periodic if $f^n(z)=z$ for some positive integer $n$. Its least period is the smallest such integer. Its orbit is the finite set of its successive states, with the cyclic order supplied by $f$. Choosing another starting state on that orbit does not give a different orbit.

If $w\in\widetilde{\mathcal A}$ lifts a state fixed by $f^n$, there is a unique integer $k$ such that

$$F^n(w)=w+(k,0).$$

Call $k$ the boundary winding over these $n$ collisions, and $k/n$ their mean boundary advance. Equivalently, $k$ is the sum of the $n$ positive boundary-arc advances, divided by $L$. It is independent of the chosen lift of the state and of the starting collision. Each normalized advance lies strictly between zero and one, so $0<k<n$. This boundary winding does not require choosing a point inside a possibly self-intersecting polygon.

Lemma (least period). If $\gcd(k,n)=1$ and $F^n(w)=w+(k,0)$, then $z=\pi(w)$ has least period $n$.

Proof. Let $d$ be its least period. Division with remainder in $n$ shows that $d$ divides $n$. Write $F^d(w)=w+(r,0)$ for an integer $r$. Equivariance under integer translation gives $k=(n/d)r$. The integer $n/d$ divides both $k$ and $n$, so it equals one. Thus $d=n$. Without coprimality, the displayed equation alone does not establish the least period.

The periodic-orbit theorem

The following form of the Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem is used as an external theorem. Its conclusion counts distinct orbits, not merely distinct fixed points of an iterate; see Franks, Rotation Numbers and Instability Sets, Theorem 4.1 and its billiard application, pp. 7–8.

Theorem (rigid boundary case). Suppose $g:\mathcal A\to\mathcal A$ is an orientation-preserving, area-preserving homeomorphism preserving each boundary component. Suppose it has a lift $G$ commuting with integer horizontal translations and, for real constants $a<b$, satisfying

$$G(x,0)=(x+a,0),\qquad G(x,1)=(x+b,1),\qquad a<b.$$

For relatively prime integers $k,n$, with $n>0$ and $a<k/n<b$, there are at least two distinct periodic orbits of $g$, with lifts satisfying $G^n(w)=w+(k,0)$. Each has least period $n$.

Proposition (periodic billiards). Under the table hypotheses above, every reduced fraction $k/n$ with $0<k<n$ is realized by at least two distinct periodic billiard orbits of least period $n$.

Proof. The preceding annulus construction verifies the theorem's topological and area hypotheses. The canonical lift has $a=0$ and $b=1$. Apply the theorem to $k/n$. The resulting states are interior, since boundary mean advances are zero and one. The least-period lemma applies to each orbit. Every state on each orbit satisfies the reflection law by the definition of $f$, so the corresponding $n$ flights form a closed billiard trajectory. Taking $k=1$ and $n=2,3,\ldots$ gives infinitely many periodic orbits with unbounded least period.

The shifted iterate and the counting issue Source calculation

For fixed $k,n$, define $D_{-k}(x,y)=(x-k,y)$ and $F_{k,n}=D_{-k}\circ F^n$. Its boundary displacements are

$$F_{k,n}(x,0)-(x,0)=(-k,0),\qquad F_{k,n}(x,1)-(x,1)=(n-k,0).$$

They have opposite signs precisely when $0<k<n$. Multiplication by $2\pi$ recovers the rotations in Birkhoff's pp. 176–177. The integer shift is invisible on the annulus; it selects a particular winding class in its covering space.

The familiar two-fixed-point statement applied to this shifted iterate gives periodic states. Two such states could be successive collisions on a single orbit. The distinct-orbit conclusion above uses the stronger periodic-orbit formulation explicitly. Birkhoff separates the orbits by opposite fixed-point indices. An isolated fixed-point index is the winding of the nonzero displacement vector along a small surrounding loop; no such isolating loop exists for a point on a continuous circle of fixed points. The theorem stated above also covers that degeneracy, without assigning individual indices there.

Reversal and the number of trajectories

An involution is a map whose square is the identity. In angle coordinates the reversal involution is $R(s,\theta)=(s,\pi-\theta)$: reverse the incoming velocity at a collision to obtain the outgoing velocity for the reversed trajectory. In normalized annulus coordinates, $R(x,y)=(x,1-y)$. The reflection law gives

$$R\circ f\circ R=f^{-1}.$$

Indeed, starting from the reversed state and making one flight reaches the preceding collision with its reversed state. Thus $fR=Rf^{-1}$. Also $I=R\circ f$ satisfies $I^2=\mathrm{id}$, and $f=R\circ I$: the map is a product of two involutions, as in the source.

On an $n$-periodic trajectory, reversal replaces each positive boundary advance $\delta$ by $1-\delta$, in reverse order. It therefore replaces $k$ by $n-k$. Call two closed collision sequences equivalent as unoriented trajectories when they differ by a cyclic change of starting collision or by reversal.

For coprime $k,n$ with $n>2$ and $k<n/2$, the two orbits supplied at winding $k$ cannot be reversals of one another, since reversal has winding $n-k\ne k$. They give at least two unoriented trajectories, and their reversals give two further oriented orbits. This is the precise sense of the source's two harmonic polygons and four periodic motions. Here a harmonic polygon means a closed polygonal trajectory satisfying the reflection law; it need not be simple.

For $k/n=1/2$ in lowest terms, $n=2$. Each orbit is a segment traversed back and forth. Reversal fixes these normal-incidence states and does not create a new orbit. There are at least two such trajectories. For the ellipse $x(t)=(a\cos t,b\sin t)$ with $a>b>0$, the major and minor axes give explicit distinct examples: their endpoints have normals parallel to the corresponding axis, and normal incidence reverses the velocity.

Example: a five-collision star in a circle

For a circle of radius $r>0$, the explicit circle map has $s_1=s+2r\theta\pmod{2\pi r}$ and $\theta_1=\theta$. Its canonical lift therefore advances $x=s/(2\pi r)$ by $\theta/\pi$ at every collision. Choose $\theta=2\pi/5$. Five steps advance $x$ by two; coprimality gives least period five. The reversed orbit has angle $3\pi/5$ and winding three.

There is a continuum of starting positions at this angle, partitioned into five-state orbits. This illustrates why isolated fixed-point indices are not a general substitute for the periodic-orbit theorem. By contrast, if $\theta/\pi$ is irrational, the orbit is recurrent but never periodic: the recurrence theorem alone cannot supply the rational winding conclusion.

Near-grazing motion and a curvature-weighted invariant

Let $\kappa:\mathbb R/L\mathbb Z\to(0,\infty)$ be the curvature, defined by $\tau'(s)=\kappa(s)n(s)$, and let $\kappa'=d\kappa/ds$. For $\theta>0$ small, choose the next arclength lift $s_1=s+h$ with $h>0$ small. The notation $O(\theta^j)$ below means a remainder bounded by a constant times $\theta^j$, uniformly in $s$, as $\theta\to0^+$.

Proposition (grazing expansion). The collision map satisfies

$$h=\frac{2\theta}{\kappa}-\frac{4\kappa'}{3\kappa^3}\theta^2+O(\theta^3),\qquad \theta_1-\theta=\frac{2\kappa'}{3\kappa^2}\theta^2+O(\theta^3),$$

where the curvature and its derivative on the right are evaluated at $s$.

Derivation from the tangent and normal frame Proof

The Frenet equations $\tau'=\kappa n$ and $n'=-\kappa\tau$ give

$$\gamma(s+h)-\gamma(s)=\left(h-\frac{\kappa^2h^3}{6}\right)\tau(s)+\left(\frac{\kappa h^2}{2}+\frac{\kappa'h^3}{6}\right)n(s)+O(h^4).$$

The angle of this chord with $\tau(s)$ is therefore $\theta=\kappa h/2+\kappa'h^2/6+O(h^3)$. Inversion gives the stated formula for $h$. The tangent turns through $\int_s^{s+h}\kappa(u)\,du$ between the endpoints. Reflection gives

$$\theta_1=\int_s^{s+h}\kappa(u)\,du-\theta=\kappa h+\tfrac12\kappa'h^2-\theta+O(h^3).$$

Substituting $h$ proves the second formula.

Define $J(s,\theta)=\theta\kappa(s)^{-1/3}$. This is an approximate invariant, in the following precise one-step sense:

$$J(s_1,\theta_1)-J(s,\theta)=O(\theta^3).$$

To verify it, put $c(s)=\kappa(s)^{-1/3}$. Taylor expansion gives $c(\theta_1-\theta)+c'\theta h+O(\theta^3)$. Its quadratic coefficient is

$$\frac{2}{3}\kappa'\kappa^{-7/3}-\frac{2}{3}\kappa'\kappa^{-7/3}=0.$$

More generally, replacing $c$ by a positive twice continuously differentiable weight gives the equation $c'/c=-\kappa'/(3\kappa)$ for cancellation. This determines the curvature exponent, up to a constant factor. The formal differential equation $d\theta/ds=(\kappa'/3\kappa)\theta$ on Birkhoff's p. 179 expresses the same cancellation, with approximate level curves $\theta=J\kappa^{1/3}$.

Lazutkin coordinates. Set

$$C_\kappa=\int_0^L\kappa(u)^{2/3}\,du.$$

$$X(s)=\frac{1}{C_\kappa}\int_0^s\kappa(u)^{2/3}\,du\pmod1,$$

$$Y(s,\theta)=\frac{4}{C_\kappa}\kappa(s)^{-1/3}\sin(\theta/2).$$

These coordinates are defined on a sufficiently small collar $0\le\theta<\varepsilon$ of the grazing boundary. The leading relation $Y=2J/C_\kappa+O(\theta^3)$ identifies the approximate invariant with a modern transverse coordinate. Using the increasing real lift of $X$ along the short boundary arc, $X(s_1)-X(s)=2J/C_\kappa+O(\theta^2)$: the same choice makes the leading horizontal advance independent of position. Thus the source's two powers of curvature serve complementary purposes: one cancels transverse drift, the other normalizes longitudinal speed.

Writing $(X_1,Y_1)$ for the coordinates of the next collision, the fuller Lazutkin normal form is $X_1=X+Y+O(Y^3)$, $Y_1=Y+O(Y^4)$; see Zhang, equations (4)–(6), p. 3, with his radius variable $\rho=1/\kappa$. These sharper orders are invoked from that normal form, not established by the second-order calculation above. The coordinates preserve a weighted area: direct differentiation gives $Y\,dX\wedge dY=(4/C_\kappa^3)\sin\theta\,ds\wedge d\theta$; ordinary $dX\,dY$ is not the original invariant measure.

The approximate collision count Differential model

Treat the collision index as a continuous variable $m$ and keep $J>0$ constant in the leading approximation. The model is

$$\frac{ds}{dm}=2J\kappa(s)^{-2/3},\qquad \Delta m=\frac{1}{2J}\int_0^L\kappa(s)^{2/3}\,ds=\frac{C_\kappa}{2J}.$$

Here $\Delta m$ is the modeled number of collisions for one circuit. If $K=\max_s\kappa(s)$, then $C_\kappa\le LK^{2/3}$. This recovers the source's curvature bound after its boundary-length normalization. The computation concerns the differential model; error accumulation for the actual discrete orbit requires an additional argument.

A one-step remainder estimate does not itself prove the existence of an exactly invariant curve or control arbitrarily many iterations. In particular, the formal calculation does not prove the source's proposed accumulation of periodic motions near the boundary. It identifies the approximate invariant and the coordinate system in which such questions can be studied.

Relation to the source

Birkhoff's pp. 176–179 pass from annulus dynamics to periodic billiard trajectories and then to a formal near-boundary calculation. The reconstruction makes the lift and winding conventions explicit, separates least period from the number of geometrically distinct trajectories, and spells out the effect of reversal. This requires the language of annulus lifts and an external periodic-point theorem; the existence theorem itself is not proved here.

The grazing expansion explains the two curvature powers in the source's differential model: one cancels the leading transverse drift, the other normalizes longitudinal advance. Lazutkin coordinates make this relationship explicit, at the cost of additional coordinate changes and asymptotic notation. The calculation establishes a one-step approximate invariant, not an invariant curve or long-time control. The sharper normal form is cited separately, and the source's stability cross-reference and proposed periodic accumulation remain untreated.
