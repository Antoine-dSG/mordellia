Original

1. Periodic motions near generalized equilibrium ($m=1$).

Poincaré's last geometric theorem and modifications thereof* yield an additional instrument for establishing the existence of periodic motions. Up to the present time no proper generalization of this theorem to higher dimensions has been found, so that its application remains limited to dynamical systems with two degrees of freedom. It is our aim in this chapter to give some of the fundamental ideas involved in the theorem and its application.

It will be remembered that motion near to a periodic motion of a Hamiltonian or Pfaffian system, with $m$ degrees of freedom and not involving the time explicitly, can be reduced to that of a similar system with only $m-1$ degrees of freedom but with an independent variable involved of period $2\pi$. Here the periodic motion itself appears as generalized equilibrium. This reduction is accomplished by means of an analytic device (chapter IV, section 1).

In the present section we shall take up the question of the existence of motions with period $2k\pi$ near the position of generalized equilibrium for a single degree of freedom. We shall prove the existence of infinitely many such nearby periodic motions in the general stable case by a process of reasoning which, while not employing Poincaré's geometric theorem explicitly, is precisely that which establishes the theorem in certain simple cases. Later (section 3) these results are interpreted with reference to the original dynamical problem with two degrees of freedom.

  • See my paper, An Extension of Poincaré's Last Geometric Theorem, Acta Mathematica, vol. 47 (1926).

Let the single pair of variables be $p,q$, so that the Hamiltonian function $H$ involves $p,q,t$, being periodic in $t$ of period $2\pi$, and vanishes at the origin $p=q=0$, together with its first partial derivatives, for all values of $t$.

If then $(p_0,q_0)$ is any point near to the origin, there is a unique solution $$p=f(p_0,q_0,t),\qquad q=g(p_0,q_0,t)$$ which for $t=0$ takes on the values $p_0,q_0$, and which is analytic in $p_0,q_0,t$ for $t$ arbitrarily large and $p_0,q_0$ sufficiently small. Let $p_1,q_1$ denote the values of $p,q$ after a complete period $2\pi$. Evidently we have $$p_1=f(p_0,q_0,2\pi),\qquad q_1=g(p_0,q_0,2\pi)$$ where $f$ and $g$ are analytic in $p_0,q_0$, and vanish with these variables.

In this way a transformation $T$ is defined, of the same nature as the transformation of the surface of section obtained in the preceding chapter (section 10). For if we write $r=t$, the pair of Hamiltonian equations may be replaced by the equivalent set $$\frac{dp}{dt}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},\qquad \frac{dq}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\qquad \frac{dr}{dt}=1,$$ in which $H$ is a function of $p,q,r$ of period $2\pi$ in $r$. Here the manifold of states of motion is the three-dimensional $p,q,r$ space in which the $r$ axis represents a periodic motion, namely that corresponding to generalized equilibrium; it must not be forgotten that $r$ is an angular variable. Now $\varphi=r=0$ will serve as a surface of section according to our earlier work, although here we are limited to a certain neighborhood. A point $(p_0,q_0,0)$ in this surface of section is taken along its stream line to $(p_1,q_1,2\pi)$, i. e., to $(p_1,q_1,0)$. Thus the transformation written above is indeed a transformation $T$ of a surface of section $S$ which is, however, only locally defined. Such ‘local surfaces of section’ can of course be constructed near a periodic motion in any dynamical problem by merely taking an element of surface which intersects but is not tangent to the corresponding stream line in the manifold of states of motion.

Now the fluid motion defined by the above three equations is that of an incompressible fluid, since the divergence of the right-hand members is $0$. Consequently if we follow any tube of fluid made of sections of stream lines between the parallel planes $r=0$ and $r=2\pi$, which will move constantly with unit velocity in the $r$ direction, we infer that the loss of volume at one base in time $\Delta t$ is nearly $\sigma_0\Delta t$ ($\sigma_0$, area of first base), while the equal gain at the other is nearly $\sigma_1\Delta t$ ($\sigma_1$, area of second base). By allowing $\Delta t$ to approach $0$ we infer that $\sigma_0=\sigma_1$.

Since $\sigma_0$ is an arbitrary area in $r=0$, it is clear that $T$ must be an area-preserving transformation of the variables $p_0,q_0$. This important property of $T$ corresponds to a general property of the surface transformations associated with dynamical problems.

It is necessary now to state the conditions to be imposed upon the generalized equilibrium, with the aid of which the conclusion stated may be established.

We assume in the first place that the generalized equilibrium is of general stable type, and therefore completely stable. The significance of the normal form (chapter III, section 9) is that the solution may be written $$p=p_0e^{M(p_0q_0)t}+\Phi,\qquad q=q_0e^{-M(p_0q_0)t}+\Psi,$$ in properly chosen conjugate variables $p,q$. Here $\Phi,\Psi$ are given as convergent power series in $p_0,q_0$ with initial terms of arbitrarily high degree $2\mu+1$, and with all coefficients analytic functions of $t$; these series converge absolutely and uniformly for any fixed range of values for $t$, such as $|t|\leq2\pi$, when $p_0,q_0$ are small. The function $M$ can be taken as a polynomial of degree not more than $\mu$ in the product $p_0q_0$, with pure imaginary constant coefficients, of the form $$\lambda+lp_0q_0+\cdots+sp_0^\mu q_0^\mu$$ with $\lambda$ the multiplier. By the hypothesis of stability $\lambda/\sqrt{-1}$ is not rational, and in particular is not $0$.

Our second assumption is that $l$ is not $0$. In case $l$ vanishes but some other coefficient in $M$ is not $0$, essentially the same argument would apply. Thus the only case of failure is that in which the formal series $M$ in the complete normal form reduces to a mere constant $\lambda$. This is a highly degenerate case, and actual examples can be constructed to show that an analogous conclusion cannot then be drawn.

The normal form gives a means of studying the transformation $T$. The property of the transformation $T$ necessary for our present purposes is embodied in the following lemma whose proof is deferred to the next section:

Lemma. For $l\ne0$, upon suitable choice of the variables $p,q$, the positive quantity $\varepsilon$ may be taken arbitrarily small, and then the integer $n$ so large that any transformation $T^\nu$ $(\nu\leq n)$ takes the circle $r\leq\varepsilon$ about the invariant point $r=0$ into a region within the circle of radius $2\varepsilon$, while the angular rotation effected by $T^n$ increases from $n\lambda/\sqrt{-1}$ with $r$ along any radial line for $r\leq\varepsilon$, being at least $2\pi$ greater for $r=\varepsilon$ than for $r=0$.

Let $r,\theta$ be polar coordinates and let $(r_n,\theta_n)$ denote the iterate of $(r,\theta)$ under $T^n$, where the rectangular coordinates $p,q$, the radius $\varepsilon$, and the integer $n$ are selected as in the lemma. For any fixed $\theta_0$ the difference $\theta_n-\theta_0$ will then increase from $n\lambda/\sqrt{-1}$ at $r=0$ to a quantity at least $2\pi$ greater at $r=\varepsilon$. Hence there will be a unique solution of the equation $$\theta_n-\theta_0=2k\pi$$ along a fixed radius vector, where $2k\pi$ is the least integral multiple of $2\pi$ exceeding $n\lambda/\sqrt{-1}$. Hence the analytic curve $C$ given by this equation is met once and only once by each radius sector.

Now let us consider the image $C_n$ of this curve under the transformation $T^n$; the curve $C_n$ intersects $C$ at some point $Q$, since if $C_n$ is wholly within $C$ or outside $C$, $T^n$ would not be area-preserving in the original variables. The point $Q$ is obtained from some point $P$, also on $C$, by the transformation $T^n$. Moreover $P$ and $Q$ have the same $\theta$, by definition of $C$. Thus $P$ and $Q$ must coincide, and $P$ is invariant under $T^n$. Since $\varepsilon$ is arbitrarily small, we obtain the result stated:

In the case of generalized equilibrium of general stable type for a Hamiltonian problem with one degree of freedom $(l\ne0)$, there exist infinitely many periodic motions in the vicinity.

Modernized

Periodic forcing and generalized equilibrium

A generalized equilibrium here is the solution $p=q=0$ of a periodically time-dependent system. A periodic point of the time-$2\pi$ map represents a motion whose period is an integer multiple of $2\pi$. “General stable type” refers to the nonresonant normal-form hypotheses used below, not a new assertion of nonlinear Lyapunov stability. The normal-form construction is an input from Chapter III.

Finite normal form and the twist lemma

It is necessary now to state the conditions to be imposed upon the generalized equilibrium, with the aid of which the conclusion stated may be established.

We assume in the first place that the generalized equilibrium is of general stable type, and therefore completely stable. The significance of the normal form (chapter III, section 9) is that the solution may be written $$p=p_0e^{M(p_0q_0)t}+\Phi,\qquad q=q_0e^{-M(p_0q_0)t}+\Psi,$$ in properly chosen conjugate variables $p,q$. Here $\Phi,\Psi$ are given as convergent power series in $p_0,q_0$ with initial terms of arbitrarily high degree $2\mu+1$, and with all coefficients analytic functions of $t$; these series converge absolutely and uniformly for any fixed range of values for $t$, such as $|t|\leq2\pi$, when $p_0,q_0$ are small. The function $M$ can be taken as a polynomial of degree not more than $\mu$ in the product $p_0q_0$, with purely imaginary constant coefficients, of the form $$\lambda+lp_0q_0+\cdots+sp_0^\mu q_0^\mu$$ with $\lambda$ the multiplier. By the hypothesis of stability $\lambda/i$ is not rational, and in particular is not $0$.

Our second assumption is that $l$ is not $0$. In case $l$ vanishes but some other coefficient in $M$ is not $0$, essentially the same argument would apply. Thus the only case of failure is that in which the formal series $M$ in the complete normal form reduces to a constant $\lambda$. This is a highly degenerate case, and actual examples can be constructed to show that an analogous conclusion cannot then be drawn.

The normal form gives a means of studying the transformation $T$. The property of the transformation $T$ necessary for our present purposes is embodied in the following lemma whose proof is deferred to the next section:

Lemma. For $l\ne0$, upon suitable choice of the variables $p,q$, the positive quantity $\varepsilon$ may be taken arbitrarily small, and then the integer $n$ so large that any transformation $T^\nu$ $(\nu\leq n)$ takes the circle $r\leq\varepsilon$ about the fixed point $r=0$ into a region within the circle of radius $2\varepsilon$, while the angular displacement effected by $T^n$ increases from $n\lambda/i$ with $r$ along any radial line for $r\leq\varepsilon$, being at least $2\pi$ greater for $r=\varepsilon$ than for $r=0$.

An intersection forces a periodic point

Let $r,\theta$ be polar coordinates and let $(r_n,\theta_n)$ denote the iterate of $(r,\theta)$ under $T^n$, where the rectangular coordinates $p,q$, the radius $\varepsilon$, and the integer $n$ are selected as in the lemma. For any fixed $\theta_0$ the difference $\theta_n-\theta_0$ will then increase from $n\lambda/i$ at $r=0$ to a quantity at least $2\pi$ greater at $r=\varepsilon$. Hence there will be a unique solution of the equation $$\theta_n-\theta_0=2k\pi$$ along a fixed radial ray, where $2k\pi$ is the least integral multiple of $2\pi$ exceeding $n\lambda/i$. Reinterpreted

The time-period map

Let $H(p,q,t)$ be real analytic, $2\pi$-periodic in $t$, and satisfy $H_p(0,0,t)=H_q(0,0,t)=0$. The equations are $\dot p=-H_q$, $\dot q=H_p$. For a sufficiently small neighborhood of the origin, their time-$2\pi$ map $T$ is an analytic local diffeomorphism fixing the origin. The neighborhood may depend on the finite time interval under consideration. A solution with $T^nz=z$ closes after $2\pi n$ units of time; its least period may be smaller.

The suspended vector field on $(p,q,t\bmod2\pi)$ is $(-H_q,H_p,1)$. Its section $t=0$ identifies a periodically forced problem with a return-map problem. This is a local section, not a claim that every trajectory in the energy manifold crosses it.

Hamiltonian area preservation

Set $\omega=dq\wedge dp$. A two-form assigns an alternating bilinear quantity to two tangent vectors; here it is oriented area. The Hamiltonian vector field $X_t$ satisfies $\iota_{X_t}\omega=d_{p,q}H$. Here $\iota_X\omega$ means the one-form $v\mapsto\omega(X,v)$, and $d_{p,q}$ differentiates only in the state variables. Therefore

$$\mathcal L_{X_t}\omega=d(\iota_{X_t}\omega)+\iota_{X_t}d\omega=d^2H=0.$$

The Lie derivative $\mathcal L_X$ measures the infinitesimal change of a form along a flow. Thus the flow, and in particular $T$, preserves $\omega$. This is the differential-form version of Birkhoff’s incompressible-tube calculation. In coordinates it is simply

$$\operatorname{div}(-H_q,H_p)=-H_{qp}+H_{pq}=0,\qquad\det DT=1.$$

A noncanonical change of variables can change the coordinate area element. What survives is a smooth positive invariant density, sufficient for the intersection argument below.

The finite normal-form input

An elliptic fixed point has linear eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\sigma}$ on the unit circle. Assume $\sigma/(2\pi)$ is irrational and that the first twist coefficient $s$ is nonzero. The finite-order normal-form input from Birkhoff’s earlier chapters gives, after a local analytic change of coordinates,

$$\rho_1=\rho+O(\rho^{\mu+1}),\qquad\theta_1=\theta+\sigma+s\rho+O(\rho^\mu),\qquad\rho=r^2,$$

with differentiated remainder estimates as in §2. The normalization is to arbitrarily high finite order; it does not assert convergence of an infinite normalizing series. Reverse the angular orientation if needed to take $s>0$.

Ellipticity alone is insufficient. The condition $s\ne0$ produces a radial variation of rotation, while area preservation prevents the resonant curve from escaping its image. This argument does not assert nonlinear stability solely from irrational linear eigenvalues.

Resonant curves and intersections

Lemma input. For arbitrarily small $\varepsilon>0$, choose an integer $n$ so that all iterates through $n$ stay in $r<2\varepsilon$, and the lifted angular displacement

$$D_n(r,\theta)=\theta_n(r,\theta)-\theta$$

strictly increases with $r>0$, from $n\sigma$ at $r=0$ to more than $n\sigma+2\pi$ at $r=\varepsilon$. Section 2 supplies these finite-time estimates.

Let $k$ be the smallest integer with $2\pi k>n\sigma$. Irrationality makes this inequality strict. On each ray there is exactly one solution of $D_n(r,\theta)=2\pi k$. The implicit-function theorem gives a closed radial graph $C:r=R(\theta)>0$. Every point of $C$ is carried by $T^n$ to the same ray.

Proposition. The curves $C$ and $T^nC$ intersect, and every intersection yields a fixed point of $T^n$ on $C$.

Proof. Both curves are simple and surround the origin. If disjoint, one bounds a disk strictly contained in the other. The intervening open annulus has positive measure for the smooth invariant density. This contradicts equality of the two disk measures under $T^n$. Choose $Q=T^nP\in C\cap T^nC$, with $P\in C$. The angular equation puts $P,Q$ on the same ray. A radial graph has only one point on a ray, so $P=Q$. ∎

The resulting nonzero periodic points approach the origin as $\varepsilon\to0$. A finite collection of periodic orbits cannot do this, since each has finitely many nonzero points. Hence there are infinitely many distinct periodic orbits near the origin. Their periods are unbounded: for each fixed $n$, $DT^n(0)-I$ is invertible by nonresonance, so the origin is an isolated zero of $T^n-I$.

Relation to the source

Birkhoff’s pp. 150–154 separate a return map, preserved area, a normal-form lemma and an intersection argument. The reinterpretation makes this dependency explicit and distinguishes finite-order normalization from an infinite convergent normal form. The new language of a preserved two-form costs differential forms; the final intersection proof needs only a positive invariant density and elementary planar topology. The normal-form construction remains an external input from Chapter III. The $2\pi$ normalization issue in the historical lemma is recorded in the edition notes.
