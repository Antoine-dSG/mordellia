Original

10. The geodesic problem. Construction of a transformation $TT^*$.

We turn our attention next to the problem afforded by the geodesics on an analytic surface. In order to obtain as concrete results as possible, we shall restrict the surface $S$ to be closed and convex, although it is evident that these limitations are not altogether essential for the argument which follows.

We have already established the existence of at least one closed geodesic $g$ of minimax type (chapter V, section 6), which we shall assume to be without double points. Our first assertion is that there exists a positive quantity $L$ so great that any geodesic arc of length exceeding $L$ intersects $g$ at least once (or falls along $g$). In the contrary case there would exist a sequence of geodesic arcs $g_n$ of length $L_n$ with $\lim L_n=+\infty$, such that each arc $g_n$ does not meet $g$. This fact would require that for $L_n$ large enough no part of $g_n$ is nearly coincident with $g$, since nearby geodesics meet a closed geodesic of minimax type in a succession of points separated by arcs of limited length; more precisely, it may be proved that if $P$ is any point of $g$ and $P''$ is its second conjugate point in the sense of the calculus of variations, then the arc $PP''$ constitutes at least one complete circuit of $g$.†

† For a proof see my paper, Dynamical Systems with Two Degrees of Freedom, Trans. Amer. Math. Soc., vol. 18 (1917), in particular section 19.

Hence if we let $P_n$ be the mid-point of $g_n$, the sequence of points $P_n$ will have a limiting point $P$ not on $g$, and the geodesic $g_n$ will have at least one limiting direction at $P$, such that the complete geodesic $h$ through $P$ in this direction fails to meet $g$ and indeed nowhere approaches $g$.

Consider now that part of the surface $S$ (which is divided by $g$ in two parts) upon which $h$ lies, and in particular the part of $S$ lying between $g$ and $h$. One boundary of this region $s$ is $g$, of geodesic curvature $0$ everywhere, while the other boundary $\gamma$ consists of part or all of $h$, and of its limit points.

Let $N_1$ and $N_2$ be two nearby accessible points of the boundary $\gamma$ so obtained, so that a short curvilinear arc $N_1AN_2$ exists in the region $s$ with all of its interior points not on the boundary. Consider also the short geodesic arc $N_1GN_2$. Then $N_1AN_2GN_1$ delimits a region $\sigma$.

If any interior point of $N_1GN_2$ is part of the boundary $\gamma$, all of $N_1GN_2$ must be part of it; otherwise the curve $h$ would cut across $N_1GN_2$ and so certainly intersect $N_1AN_2$, contrary to hypothesis. In this case $N_1GN_2$ is a part of the boundary $\gamma$ and the region $\sigma$ lies entirely in $s$. If there is no such interior point, the geodesic arc $N_1GN_2$ lies inside of $s$ except at $N_1$ and $N_2$. Hence if we surround the boundary $\gamma$ by a cyclical chain of nearby points

$$N_1,N_2,\ldots,N_n,$$

the short distinct geodesic arcs

$$N_1N_2,N_2N_3,\ldots,N_nN_1$$

lie inside of $s$ or coincide with $\gamma$. We assume that this chain encircles $\gamma$ in a positive angular sense: in this event, the part toward $\gamma$ will lie everywhere to the left.

Now in the geodesic polygon so formed, the angle in $s$ at any vertex will be less than or equal to $\pi$. For if the angle at $N_{i+1}$, say, exceeds $\pi$ and we consider the short geodesic arc $N_iN_{i+2}$, it is apparent that $\gamma$ lies to the left of $N_iN_{i+2}$ also, so that $N_{i+1}$ is completely encircled by points not on $\gamma$ which is impossible.

But the integral curvature of the part of $S$ bounded by this polygon will be precisely the sum of these $n$ interior angles diminished by $(n-2)\pi$ by a well known formula, and so will be less than $2\pi$, which is impossible since the integral curvature of either part of $S$ bounded by $g$ is exactly $2\pi$ by the same formula.

Thus a contradiction has been obtained, so that the original assertion must be true.

We now introduce parameters as follows. Let an arbitrary directed geodesic $f$ cut the directed geodesic $g$ in a point $P$. The position of $P$ may be measured by means of the arc length $\theta$ from a fixed point $P_0$ of $g$; if the total length of $g$ be taken as $2\pi$ by an appropriate choice of a unit of length, the variable $\theta$ is a periodic variable of period $2\pi$. Furthermore let $\varphi$ denote the angle between the positive directions of $g$ and $f$ at the point $P$ so that $0<\varphi<\pi$.

The next crossing of $g$ by $h$ will be in the opposite sense, and the corresponding $\theta_1^*$ and $\varphi_1^*$ will vary analytically with $\theta$ and $\varphi$. Thus a transformation $T$ is defined

$$T:\quad\varphi_1^*=f(\varphi,\theta),\quad\theta_1^*=g(\varphi,\theta),$$

which takes the ring

$$R:\quad0<\varphi<\pi,\quad0\le\theta<2\pi$$

($\varphi,\theta$, polar coördinates) into itself.

Similarly a crossing in the opposite sense at $\varphi^*,\theta^*$ will be followed by a crossing $\varphi_1,\theta_1$ thus defining a second one-to-one, analytic transformation

$$T^*:\quad\varphi_1=f^*(\varphi^*,\theta^*),\quad\theta_1=g^*(\varphi^*,\theta^*)$$

of $R$ into itself.

Along and near the boundaries of $R$, $T$ and $T^*$ are to be regarded as continuous. This fact may be seen as follows: If a geodesic $f$ near to $g$ intersects $g$ at a small angle $\varphi$ with given $\theta$, then of course $f$ will intersect later at a small angle $\varphi_1^*$ with coördinate $\theta_1^*$ nearly that of the conjugate point to the first point of intersection, as was observed above. Obviously this ensures the specified continuity. Furthermore, we know that three successive conjugate points correspond to an arc of more than one complete cycle of $g$.

If we have $\varphi=0$ or $\pi$ we have respectively $\varphi_1^*=0$ or $\pi$, while if $\varphi^*=0$ or $\pi$ we have likewise $\varphi_1=0$ or $\pi$. Furthermore as $\theta$ or $\theta^*$ increases along a boundary so will $\theta_1^*$ or $\theta_1$; in fact it has been noted that if $\theta$ is the coördinate of $P$, then $\theta_1^*$ is the coördinate of the conjugate point $P'$. Hence $T$ and $T^*$ are direct transformations leaving the outer and inner boundaries of $R$ invariant.

It is now desirable to consider somewhat more in detail the nature of $T$ and $T^*$ along the boundaries. These transformations are evidently not determined up to a complete rotation, and it is desirable to make a convention which eliminates this arbitrary factor, and enables us to compare the transformations along the inner and outer boundaries. Let us consider any directed geodesic arc $P_0P_1$ of $f$ between two successive crossings $P_0$ and $P_1$ of $g$ by $f$, and let us define a double point $Q$ of $P_0P_1$ as positive if a moving point in describing the arc $P_0P_1$ passes over the arc $P_0Q$ already described at $Q$, from the left to the right side; and as negative in the contrary case. The ‘index’ of $P_0P_1$ may be defined as the difference between the number of positive and the number of negative crossings within $P_0P_1$. Let us define the value $\theta_1^*$ of $\theta$ at $P_1$ as that corresponding to the positively taken arc $P_0P_1$ increased by $2\pi i$ where $i$ is the index.

We can now show that, as thus defined, $\theta_1^*$ varies continuously with $\theta$. In fact as $\theta$ and $\varphi$ change, no new crossings can be introduced within $P_0P_1$ inasmuch as a geodesic arc cannot be tangent to itself. If a positive crossing is introduced at $P_1$, evidently $\theta_1^*$ increases through an exact multiple of $2\pi$ as it should. If a negative crossing is introduced, $\theta_1^*$ decreases similarly. Thus the convention is appropriate to all cases.

Now it is also evident that if a geodesic arc $P_0P_1$ near to $g$ be continuously deformed near $g$ while not maintaining its geodesic character, with $P_0$ and $P_1$ fixed, and only simple interior tangency allowed, then positive and negative crossings appear in or disappear in associated pairs. Thus if the arc $P_0P_1$ be deformed into a spiral so as to eliminate all negative crossings, it is plain that the index will equal the number of apparent circuits of $g$ made by the spiral. In this case the increase in $\theta$, namely $\theta_1^*-\theta$, will be measured by the arc length along the curve $g$, according to our convention. However if the positive crossings are eliminated, the convention gives $2\pi i$ $(i\le0)$ increased by the angular value of the short positive arc $P_0P_1$ as the difference $\theta_1^*-\theta$, and hence this difference exceeds the arc length $P_0P_1$ by $2\pi$.

Consequently it is seen that $\theta_1^*-\theta$ is measured in the sense of the convention by the actual increase in $\theta$ along $g$ for $\varphi=0$, while, for $\varphi=\pi$, $\theta_1^*-\theta$ is measured by the algebraic increase along the arc (actually a decrease) augmented by $2\pi$ since $P_0P_1$ is to be taken positively.

Now in going from a point $P$ to its second conjugate point $P''$ with $\varphi=0$, $\theta$ increases by an amount $\alpha$,

$$2k\pi<\alpha<2(k+1)\pi,$$

where $k\ge1$ is independent of the position of $P$; in fact the one-to-one, direct transformation of the points of $g$ from $P$ to $P''$ defines a rotation number‡ which lies between $2k\pi$ and $2(k+1)\pi$ with $k\ge1$, because of a property of the conjugate points along a geodesic of minimax type already specified. Hence we infer

$$2k\pi\le g(0,\theta)-\theta<2(k+1)\pi.$$

Similarly if we consider $\varphi=\pi$, then $\theta_1^*-2\pi$ is diminished by a like amount so that we find

$$-2k\pi<g(\pi,\theta)-\theta\le-2(k-1)\pi.$$

It follows that the transformation $T$ (or $T^*$) advances points in opposite angular directions along the two boundaries of $R$, at least if the exceptional case of a second conjugate point coincident with the initial point after a single circuit be excluded.

‡ For definition and short discussion of the Poincaré rotation numbers, see my paper, Surface Transformations and their Dynamical Applications, Acta Mathematica, vol. 43 (1922), section 45.

Modernized

A uniform bound before crossing

Let $S$ be a closed convex analytic surface, and let $g$ be the closed geodesic of minimax type supplied by Chapter V, §6. Assume $g$ is simple. Birkhoff asserts that there is $L>0$ such that every geodesic arc longer than $L$ either intersects $g$ or lies along it.

Suppose otherwise. There are arcs $g_n$ of lengths $L_n\to\infty$ avoiding $g$. Nearby geodesics cross $g$ repeatedly with bounded spacing, so these avoiding arcs cannot remain nearly coincident with $g$. The source invokes a variational fact: from any $P\in g$, the arc to the second conjugate point $P''$ covers at least one circuit of $g$. The reference is Birkhoff, Dynamical Systems with Two Degrees of Freedom (1917), §19.

Take midpoints of $g_n$. Compactness gives a convergent subsequence of points and tangent directions. Its limiting complete geodesic $h$ neither meets nor approaches $g$. In the component of $S\setminus g$ containing $h$, consider the region $s$ between $g$ and the boundary $\gamma$ made from $h$ and its limit points. The curve $g$ has geodesic curvature zero.

The geodesic-polygon contradiction

Choose nearby accessible points $N_1,N_2$ of $\gamma$, join them through the interior of $s$ by a short arc $N_1AN_2$, and also join them by the short geodesic $N_1GN_2$. If an interior point of the latter belongs to $\gamma$, the source argues that the whole short geodesic belongs to $\gamma$: otherwise $h$ would cross it and hence cross the chosen interior arc. If there is no such interior point, the geodesic lies in $s$ apart from its endpoints.

A cyclic chain $N_1,\ldots,N_n$ therefore produces a geodesic polygon lying in $s$ or on $\gamma$, with the side toward $\gamma$ on the left. Its angles on the $s$ side are at most $\pi$. Indeed, if the angle at $N_{i+1}$ exceeded $\pi$, the short chord $N_iN_{i+2}$ would still leave $\gamma$ to the left, enclosing $N_{i+1}$ by points outside $\gamma$, a contradiction.

The curvature formula used here is the geodesic-polygon case of Gauss–Bonnet: total curvature equals the sum of interior angles minus $(n-2)\pi$. Birkhoff concludes that the polygon construction gives curvature less than $2\pi$, in contradiction with the value $2\pi$ for either region bounded by the simple closed geodesic $g$. He thereby concludes the uniform-crossing assertion. This preserves his limiting-boundary and polygon argument; its boundary regularity is not replaced by an independent proof here.

The two crossing maps

Normalize the length of $g$ to $2\pi$. Write $s\in\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$ for arclength and $\beta\in(0,\pi)$ for the angle of a geodesic departing to one side of $g$. Thus $(s,\beta)$ lies in an annulus. The next crossing is in the opposite sense and gives a one-to-one analytic map

$$T:(s,\beta)\longmapsto(s_1^*,\beta_1^*).$$

Starting on that opposite side defines another map

$$T^*:(s^*,\beta^*)\longmapsto(s_1,\beta_1).$$

Both crossing spaces are represented by the same annulus in the source, but the star keeps track of which side is being crossed. Following both maps gives a return with the original crossing sense. This is Birkhoff's $TT^*$, written in his order of successive application.

Boundary values and conjugate points

As the departure angle tends to zero, the next intersection approaches the first conjugate point along $g$ and the next angle tends to zero. The analogous statement holds near angle $\pi$. This is the source's argument for continuity at the two boundary circles.

The conjugate-point coordinate increases as the starting coordinate increases. Consequently $T$ and $T^*$ preserve orientation and each boundary circle. The source also uses the minimax information about three successive conjugate points spanning more than one circuit. The exceptional equality after one circuit is excluded in the final twist assertion.

Fixing a real lift by self-crossings

Endpoint coordinates modulo $2\pi$ do not specify a real displacement. Birkhoff chooses a lift using the geodesic segment between successive crossings $P_0,P_1$ of $g$. Give a self-crossing sign $+1$ when the moving point crosses the previously traced branch from its left to its right, and sign $-1$ otherwise. Let $i$ be the sum of these signs.

Take the positively measured boundary arc from $P_0$ to $P_1$ and add $2\pi i$ to define the lifted endpoint displacement. The source argues that this varies continuously: an interior self-tangency cannot occur for a geodesic; when a positive or negative crossing passes through the endpoint, the integer correction changes in precisely the direction needed to continue the endpoint coordinate through a multiple of $2\pi$.

This “index” counts signed self-crossings of an arc; it is different from the fixed-point index in §11.

The boundary twist argument

Deform a near-boundary segment with its endpoints fixed, allowing simple interior tangencies. Positive and negative self-crossings are then created or removed in pairs. Eliminating the negative crossings leaves a spiral whose index counts its positive circuits. Eliminating the positive crossings gives the corresponding negative count, together with the positive short boundary arc.

It follows, in Birkhoff's convention, that the displacement on the angle-zero boundary is the actual forward advance along $g$. On the angle-$\pi$ boundary it is the backward advance plus $2\pi$.

The source next considers advance $\alpha$ to the second conjugate point and writes

$$2k\pi<\alpha<2(k+1)\pi,\qquad k\ge1.$$

It invokes the rotation number of the orientation-preserving conjugate-point map to choose $k$ independently of the starting point. Retaining the source's notation for its endpoint function as $b(\beta,s)$, its resulting bounds are

$$2k\pi\le b(0,s)-s<2(k+1)\pi,$$

$$-2k\pi<b(\pi,s)-s\le-2(k-1)\pi.$$

Birkhoff concludes opposite boundary directions for $T$ or $T^*$, excluding the exceptional case in which the second conjugate point is the starting point after exactly one circuit. His reference for rotation numbers is Surface Transformations and their Dynamical Applications (1922), §45.

Reinterpreted

Crossing annuli

Let $(S,h)$ be a smooth oriented Riemannian surface diffeomorphic to the two-sphere, with Gaussian curvature $K:S\to(0,\infty)$. Let $\gamma:\mathbb R/L\mathbb Z\to S$ be a simple closed unit-speed geodesic of length $L>0$. A geodesic has zero covariant acceleration, $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0$, where $\nabla$ is the Levi-Civita connection of the metric $h$. The existence of this chosen simple closed geodesic is an input. These intrinsic hypotheses replace the source’s analytic convex surface; strict positivity of $K$ is explicit.

Write $e(s)=\dot\gamma(s)$ and let $n(s)$ be its positive perpendicular unit vector in the tangent plane of $S$. The embedded curve $\gamma$ separates $S$ into two disks; label the side toward $n$ by $+$.

The unit tangent bundle is $T^1S=\{(q,v):q\in S,\ v\in T_qS,\ h_q(v,v)=1\}$. A state records position and velocity. For $\sigma\in\{+1,-1\}$ define

$$\iota_\sigma(s,\theta)=\bigl(\gamma(s),\cos\theta\,e(s)+\sigma\sin\theta\,n(s)\bigr),\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi.$$

Its image $A_\sigma$ is a closed annulus in $T^1S$. The interiors $A_\sigma^\circ$ consist of states crossing into the corresponding side. Their shared boundary consists of the two directed tangent orbits $(\gamma(s),\pm e(s))$. In particular, $\theta$ on $A_-$ is a positive angle toward $-n$, not the signed angle toward $n$.

Let $\Phi^t:T^1S\to T^1S$ denote unit-speed geodesic flow: $\Phi^t(q,v)=(\eta(t),\dot\eta(t))$, where $\eta(0)=q$ and $\dot\eta(0)=v$. Compactness of $S$ makes this flow defined for every real $t$. Unlike the billiard map, a crossing does not reflect velocity.

Two crossings make one return

For $z\in A_\sigma^\circ$ define the next-crossing time

$$\tau_\sigma(z)=\inf\{t>0:\Phi^t(z)\in A_{-\sigma}^\circ\},$$

with value $+\infty$ if there is no crossing. Positive curvature supplies the essential global input.

Theorem (Birkhoff, invoked). Under the hypotheses above, both next-crossing times are finite on their entire open annuli. They extend smoothly and positively to the closed annuli, as do the crossing maps, which are diffeomorphisms. The boundary times are specified by conjugate points below. See Abbondandolo, Bramham, Hryniewicz and Salomão, Theorem 2.1, Proposition 2.2 and Corollary 2.3.

Define $T_\sigma:A_\sigma^\circ\to A_{-\sigma}^\circ$ and the same-side return map $F:A_+^\circ\to A_+^\circ$ by

$$T_\sigma(z)=\Phi^{\tau_\sigma(z)}(z),\qquad F=T_-\circ T_+.$$

The elapsed time of this return is the function $\tau:A_+^\circ\to(0,\infty)$ given by

$$\tau(z)=\tau_+(z)+\tau_-(T_+(z)),\qquad F(z)=\Phi^{\tau(z)}(z).$$

The composition is written in modern right-to-left order. It is the same-side operation denoted $TT^*$ in the source, rather than a single crossing.

Local proof of smoothness. Near an intersection, take a signed normal coordinate $r$ with $\gamma=\{r=0\}$. The arrival condition is $r(\eta_z(t))=0$. Its time derivative is nonzero: a geodesic tangent to $\gamma$ at an intersection would coincide with $\gamma$ by uniqueness of the geodesic equation, contradicting the initial transverse state. The implicit function theorem therefore determines the arrival time smoothly near each interior state. Tracing the geodesic backward supplies the inverse. This argument explains regularity once a crossing exists; it does not prove that a crossing must occur.

The curvature mechanism in the global return theorem

For a disk $D$ with smooth boundary, Gauss–Bonnet reads $\int_D K\,dA_h+\int_{\partial D}k_g\,ds=2\pi$, where $dA_h$ is metric area and $k_g$ is signed geodesic curvature. Each disk bounded by $\gamma$ consequently has total curvature $2\pi$.

For a disk bounded by a simple geodesic $m$-gon with interior angles $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m$, the corresponding formula is

$$\int_D K\,dA_h=\sum_{i=1}^m\alpha_i-(m-2)\pi.$$

Thus angles at most $\pi$ give a curvature budget at most $2\pi$. Birkhoff’s contradiction constructs a polygon around a hypothetical limiting geodesic avoiding $\gamma$; the disk on the appropriate side contains a full $\gamma$-hemisphere and additional positive curvature, exceeding that budget. The compactness argument and construction around the possibly nonsmooth limiting set are the global part of the theorem, not consequences of the local implicit-function calculation. The original argument occupies pp. 180–181; the global theorem above is used as an external input.

Since the extended times are continuous on compact annuli, there is a uniform finite upper bound for their values. This is stronger than merely having a return for each fixed transverse state.

Conjugate points and tangent limits

For $s\in\mathbb R/L\mathbb Z$ and $\varepsilon\in\{+1,-1\}$, the tangent geodesic is $t\mapsto\gamma(s+\varepsilon t)$. Define $j_s^\varepsilon:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ by the scalar initial-value problem

$$\ddot j_s^\varepsilon(t)+K(\gamma(s+\varepsilon t))j_s^\varepsilon(t)=0,\qquad j_s^\varepsilon(0)=0,\quad\dot j_s^\varepsilon(0)=1.$$

A dot denotes differentiation in elapsed arclength $t$. This is the normal Jacobi equation: if nearby unit-speed geodesics start at the same point with a small change of initial angle, their first-order normal separation is the angle change multiplied by $j_s^\varepsilon(t)$. It follows by differentiating the geodesic equation with respect to that initial angle; in a parallel normal frame, the curvature term becomes multiplication by Gaussian curvature.

Let $c_1^\varepsilon(s)<c_2^\varepsilon(s)$ be its first two positive zeros. Their endpoints are the first and second conjugate points to the starting point along this directed geodesic. Zeros are simple: a simultaneous zero of $j$ and $\dot j$ would force the whole solution to vanish. Since $K$ has a positive minimum, Sturm comparison with a positive constant-curvature equation ensures that these zeros exist.

Proposition (boundary formulas). In the annulus coordinates $(s,\theta)$, both $T_+$ and $T_-$ extend at their respective boundaries as

$$T_\sigma(s,0)=\bigl(s+c_1^+(s)\pmod L,0\bigr),$$

$$T_\sigma(s,\pi)=\bigl(s-c_1^-(s)\pmod L,\pi\bigr).$$

The full return extends as

$$F(s,0)=\bigl(s+c_2^+(s)\pmod L,0\bigr),$$

$$F(s,\pi)=\bigl(s-c_2^-(s)\pmod L,\pi\bigr).$$

To see the first-zero mechanism, let $r(t,\theta)$ be the signed normal displacement of a nearby geodesic. Near the positively directed tangent orbit, $r(t,\theta)=\theta j_s^+(t)+O(\theta^2)$ on a fixed compact time interval. Divide the crossing equation by $\theta$ before taking $\theta\to0$. At $c_1^+(s)$ its limiting time derivative is nonzero, so the implicit function theorem locates a nearby zero. Positivity before that zero excludes an earlier crossing; near $t=0$ use $r(t,\theta)=t\sin\theta+O(t^2\sin\theta)$. The other side and direction follow in the same way.

After the first crossing the normal Jacobi solution has negative derivative. Restarting it with a nonzero rescaling does not change its next zero, so two successive crossings reach $c_2$, rather than $2c_1$ in general. This also explains the boundary-time formulas $\tau(s,0)=c_2^+(s)$ and $\tau(s,\pi)=c_2^-(s)$.

At a tangent state the geodesic stays on $\gamma$ for all time. The displayed boundary map is therefore a limit of transverse returns, not the infimum of positive times at which that tangent orbit lies on $\gamma$.

Why a conjugate endpoint moves monotonically

Lift arclength to $\mathbb R$ and put $k(r)=K(\gamma(r))$. For each real $s$, let $u(s,r)$ solve $u_{rr}+k(r)u=0$ with $u(s,s)=0$ and $u_r(s,s)=1$. Let $C(s)>s$ be a fixed successive zero, so $u(s,C(s))=0$.

Let $a(s,r)$ solve the same equation with initial data $(a,a_r)=(1,0)$ at $r=s$. Differentiating the initial data shows $u_s=-a$. The Wronskian $a u_r-a_r u$ is constantly $1$. At $r=C(s)$, it gives $a(s,C(s))u_r(s,C(s))=1$. Implicit differentiation now yields

$$C'(s)=-\frac{u_s(s,C(s))}{u_r(s,C(s))}=\frac{1}{u_r(s,C(s))^2}>0.$$

Thus the first and second conjugate-endpoint maps preserve circular order. Reversing the arclength coordinate gives the same conclusion for the negatively directed boundary. This proves boundary monotonicity, not opposite boundary displacement.

Lifts and the boundary twist condition

Use the annulus-lift convention: a lift $\widetilde F=(X,Y)$ acts on $\mathbb R\times[0,\pi]$ and satisfies $X(s+L,\theta)=X(s,\theta)+L$. Any other lift adds the same integer multiple of $L$ to $X$ everywhere. Define its two boundary displacements by

$$d_0(s)=X(s,0)-s,\qquad d_\pi(s)=X(s,\pi)-s.$$

Definition. Strict opposite boundary twist means $d_0(s)>0>d_\pi(s)$ for every $s$, or the reversed pair of inequalities, for one and the same lift. This differs from requiring a nonzero derivative $\partial X/\partial\theta$ throughout the annulus.

The modulo-$L$ endpoint formulas alone do not determine these displacements. In general they give

$$d_0(s)=c_2^+(s)+m_0L,\qquad d_\pi(s)=-c_2^-(s)+m_\pi L,$$

for integers $m_0,m_\pi$ fixed by a continuous lift. Changing that lift shifts both integers equally; they cannot be chosen independently to manufacture opposite signs.

In pp. 183–184, the signed number $i$ of transverse self-crossings of a geodesic segment corrects its positive endpoint arc $a\in[0,L)$ to $a+iL$. A positive crossing is one at which the later traversal crosses the earlier arc from its left side to its right side. When an endpoint passes a crossing, the jump in the representative arc is offset by the integer correction. This supplies the missing full turns. The source’s topological continuation argument, including limiting nongeneric configurations, is distinct from the differential equation for conjugate points.

An explicit normalization when crossing arcs are simple

Assume additionally that $\min K/\max K>1/4$. Under this pinching hypothesis, each crossing segment is embedded with distinct endpoints and its boundary limits satisfy $c_1^\pm<L$; these facts are the additional input of Lemma 2.9 and the proof of Theorem 2.8. They permit a direct construction without counting self-crossings.

For $z\in A_\sigma$, let $a_\sigma(z)\in(0,L)$ be the positively directed arclength on $\gamma$ from the starting point to the next-crossing point. Define $\rho(z)=a_+(z)+a_-(T_+z)$ on $A_+$. Then

$$X(s,\theta)=s+\rho(s,\theta)-L$$

is the first component of a continuous lift of $F$. At the positive tangent, each arc length is the forward conjugate travel time. At the negative tangent, it is $L$ minus the backward travel time. Therefore

$$d_0(s)=c_2^+(s)-L,\qquad d_\pi(s)=L-c_2^-(s).$$

In this normalization, $c_2^\pm(s)>L$ for all $s$ is sufficient for strict opposite twist. Equality gives zero displacement and fails the strict condition. These formulas use the stated extra hypothesis for the elementary lift construction; positive curvature by itself has not supplied a twist inequality.

The source uses additional information about a geodesic obtained by a minimax construction, and explicitly excludes an equality case on p. 185. That Chapter V input is not part of the crossing-map construction. Orientation preservation and existence of the return map alone do not establish strict twist.

The round sphere

Take $S=\{q\in\mathbb R^3:|q|=R\}$ with its induced metric, where $R>0$, and use the equator

$$\gamma(s)=R(\cos(s/R),\sin(s/R),0),\qquad L=2\pi R.$$

Its positive tangent is $e(s)=(-\sin(s/R),\cos(s/R),0)$ and choose $n=(0,0,1)$. For $0<\theta<\pi$, start from $q=\gamma(s)$ with velocity $v=\cos\theta\,e(s)+\sin\theta\,n$. The unit-speed geodesic and its normal coordinate are exactly

$$\eta(t)=q\cos(t/R)+Rv\sin(t/R),\qquad z(t)=R\sin\theta\sin(t/R).$$

The first crossing is at $t=\pi R$, with position $-q$ and velocity $-v$. Relative to the negative-side convention this gives

$$T_+(s,\theta)=(s+\pi R\pmod L,\theta).$$

The same formula holds for $T_-$. Consequently $F(s,\theta)=(s,\theta)$ on the annulus and $\tau=2\pi R$. Meanwhile $K=R^{-2}$ and

$$j(t)=R\sin(t/R),\qquad c_1=\pi R,\qquad c_2=2\pi R=L.$$

This is the exceptional equality case: the centered lift in the preceding calculation is the identity, so both boundary displacements are zero. A different lift adds the same whole turn at both boundaries and still cannot give strict opposite signs.

Relation to the source

The crossing annuli separate a global question—whether every transverse state returns—from the local calculation of the return map. The Jacobi equation explains the tangent limit and provides an explicit scalar computation; the Wronskian explains circular order. Lifted displacement then isolates the additional condition required for a boundary-twist argument.

The self-crossing index in the original calculation has an operational role: it retains full turns when endpoint coordinates wrap around the circle. It cannot be replaced by a principal-value angle without losing information. The round sphere shows why conjugate points, full turns, and strict twist must be kept distinct. The invariant-measure and fixed-point applications are developed in §11.
